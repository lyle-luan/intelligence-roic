# I-ROIC Thesis

> Status: Working thesis
> Updated: 2026-10-06

## 1. The object of study

I-ROIC studies **intelligence as an allocatable production factor**.

The question is not merely who owns the best model. The harder question is:

> Who can repeatedly convert intelligence into captured economic profit, and what assets make that conversion durable?

The research therefore starts downstream of the model.

A first-order system is:

    Energy → Compute → Intelligence → Intelligence Sink → Outcome → Feedback

A company may own only one layer, several layers, or the whole stack. Owning a strong model does not by itself prove that the company will capture the highest return.

## 2. Core hypotheses

### H1 — Intelligence may become abundant faster than high-value sinks

Model capability can improve while the marginal scarcity of generic intelligence falls. If so, the scarce factor moves downstream toward places where intelligence can act on something valuable.

Examples of possible scarce sinks include proprietary workflows, customer relationships, installed device bases, factories and physical assets, distribution, regulated or trusted action rights, transaction ownership, unique data and memory, organizational decision rights, and capital that can be allocated.

The research problem shifts from "who can generate intelligence?" toward "who has somewhere valuable to put it?"

### H2 — Model advantage and model moat are different things

A company can have a large temporary capability lead without possessing a durable moat.

Durability may depend on whether model use creates a self-reinforcing loop:

    Better intelligence
      → better outcomes
      → proprietary feedback / memory / data
      → better routing, product, workflow or model
      → more usage and higher-value outcomes

The moat is therefore not assumed to live inside model weights alone.

### H3 — Selling tokens may be a lower-value use than consuming them internally

The market often observes model companies through API revenue, subscription revenue and inference volume. But the owner of intelligence may have a more valuable option:

> consume intelligence internally to produce research, software, decisions, products, sales, physical work, or other high-value outputs.

This creates a distinction between an **Intelligence Seller** and an **Intelligence Allocator**.

A vertically integrated intelligence producer may rationally sell some intelligence while reserving some capacity for higher-return internal sinks.

### H4 — The best businesses may be defined by Sink Quality × Capture Rate

A high-value sink is not enough. Customers, workers, suppliers or competitors may capture most of the surplus.

The attractive company is one that combines:

1. high-value intelligence sinks;
2. low incremental cost of applying intelligence;
3. high share of the created surplus retained by the company;
4. proprietary feedback that improves the next cycle;
5. a long reinvestment runway.

### H5 — AI revolution and AI asset bubbles can coexist

AI can create enormous real economic value while some AI-linked assets are still overpriced.

Technology truth and asset-price truth are separate questions.

I-ROIC therefore asks both:

- Is intelligence producing real incremental cash flow or economic profit?
- How much of that future value is already embedded in the price?

### H6 — Existing assets can become more valuable when intelligence becomes cheaper

If intelligence becomes a cheaper complement to capital, then pre-existing assets may gain new earning power.

Potential examples include software distribution, search and advertising networks, enterprise workflows, vehicles, robots, factories, homes and IoT devices, logistics networks, customer bases, and proprietary datasets.

This is why Tesla and Xiaomi belong in the same research universe as model labs: the relevant question is not whether they are "AI companies," but whether they own large, defensible sinks for machine intelligence.

## 3. A provisional production function

A useful mental model is:

    Output ≈ Capital × Intelligence × Agency

Where:

- **Capital** supplies assets, distribution, rights, installed base and financial capacity.
- **Intelligence** supplies reasoning, generation, prediction, planning and adaptation.
- **Agency** supplies the ability to act repeatedly in workflows or the physical world.

This is not yet a literal econometric production function. It is a research lens.

Its implication is important: when intelligence becomes abundant, the return to scarce complementary capital and agency may rise.

## 4. What I-ROIC is not

I-ROIC is not:

- a benchmark leaderboard;
- a token-price comparison;
- a claim that all model companies lack moats;
- a claim that memory is automatically a moat;
- a search for one "killer app";
- a simple AI revenue multiple.

The framework must follow value from intelligence input all the way to captured economic profit.

## 5. Current strongest claim

The strongest current claim is:

> The strategic question of the AI economy is moving from intelligence production toward intelligence allocation.

If that is true, the key competitive capability becomes the ability to identify, own and continuously improve high-return Intelligence Sinks.

## 6. Open questions

- How fast does generic model capability commoditize?
- Which forms of memory are proprietary and durable rather than portable commodities?
- How should internal token consumption be valued when there is no market transaction?
- How should training compute and inference compute be treated in invested capital?
- What is the correct attribution method when AI improves a pre-existing business?
- When does a sink become saturated?
- Does cheap intelligence raise or lower industry concentration?
- Which layer captures surplus when intelligence, compute and endpoints are owned by different firms?
- How should marginal I-ROIC be measured before accounting disclosure catches up?
