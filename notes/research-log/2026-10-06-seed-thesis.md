# Research Log — Formation of the I-ROIC Thesis

Date: 2026-10-06  
Mode: Theory formation  
Status: Historical reasoning log — preserve even when canonical theory changes.

## 0. Earlier background

The I-ROIC line did not begin with the question "which model is best?"

Earlier research had already produced several important observations:

1. AI capability can be economically real while AI-linked assets are overpriced.
2. A "killer app" is not required to prove AI creates value.
3. The economic chain to observe is closer to:

       AI capability
         → product / workflow
         → revenue or cost saving
         → profit
         → capital return

4. Useful operating variables include unit intelligence cost, task horizon, human intervention, new activity created, utilization and incremental cash flow.
5. If intelligence becomes cheap, moat may migrate toward proprietary data, real-world access, judgment, distribution, brand, responsibility and ownership.
6. A provisional production lens emerged:

       Output ≈ Capital × Intelligence × Agency

These ideas set the stage for the 2026-10-06 discussion.

## 1. Trigger question: does the LLM itself have a moat?

The discussion began from a strong proposition:

> "LLM naturally has no moat; memory does."

Rather than accept the statement literally, the research reframed it.

The important distinction is between:

- temporary model advantage;
- durable economic moat.

Model capability can matter enormously without being the final location of durable rent.

The stronger version of the idea became:

> If model intelligence becomes increasingly reproducible, durable advantage may migrate to persistent context, proprietary feedback, workflows, distribution, action rights and owned assets.

Memory therefore became one candidate complement — not an automatically sufficient moat.

## 2. The internal-token question

A second question changed the direction of the framework.

Firms with compute and token capacity do not have to sell all intelligence to external users.

They can consume it internally.

That intelligence could produce:

- research;
- code;
- product design;
- operational decisions;
- customer service;
- sales;
- experiments;
- scientific work;
- autonomous physical actions.

This creates an opportunity-cost decision:

> Is the next unit of intelligence more valuable sold as a token, or deployed internally into a proprietary sink?

This made token revenue an incomplete valuation lens.

## 3. From Intelligence Producer to Intelligence Allocator

The next conceptual move was:

> Future winners may be distinguished less by who generates the most intelligence and more by who allocates intelligence most efficiently.

The relevant company capability becomes capital allocation for intelligence.

A company needs to choose:

- which model;
- which task;
- which workflow;
- which level of autonomy;
- how much validation;
- when to escalate to humans;
- where the next unit of compute earns the highest return.

This is the origin of the name **Intelligence ROIC**.

## 4. Intelligence Sink

To make the framework concrete, the idea of an **Intelligence Sink** emerged.

A sink is a place where machine intelligence can be turned into valuable action.

The working chain became:

    Energy
      → Compute
      → Intelligence
      → Intelligence Sink
      → Outcome
      → Feedback

This shifted attention downstream.

The question "who has the best model?" became less central than:

> Who owns the highest-value sinks, and how much intelligence can those sinks absorb at high marginal return?

## 5. Expanding the company universe

The original research scope included:

### AI-native companies

OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Kimi and related firms.

Question:

> What if their most valuable future product is not tokens, but internally generated high-value output?

### Traditional internet / cloud companies

Companies with their own models and large existing businesses.

Question:

> Does intelligence create a new profit pool, strengthen an existing one, or simply increase the cost of defending it?

### Open-source LLM ecosystems and compute factories

Question:

> If the model layer commoditizes, which complements capture the rent?

## 6. Why Tesla and Xiaomi were added

The framework then expanded beyond software.

If intelligence needs a sink, companies with large physical endpoint networks may possess valuable complements.

Tesla was added as a research case because intelligence can potentially act through vehicles, manufacturing, robotics, energy systems and internal engineering.

Xiaomi was added because intelligence can potentially act through a broad set of consumer and physical endpoints such as smartphones, vehicles, home devices and IoT systems.

No investment conclusion was made.

The important theoretical change was:

> A company does not need to sell models to be an I-ROIC company.

It may instead own the physical or digital surface on which intelligence earns a return.

## 7. High-quality sink = value creation plus capture

A further refinement was necessary.

A sink can create large social value while shareholders capture little of it.

Therefore sink analysis needs at least:

- value density;
- proprietary access;
- actionability;
- validation cost;
- feedback richness;
- capture rate;
- repeatability;
- reinvestment runway.

This connects technology to valuation.

## 8. Recursive feedback

The most interesting possible moat is not simply a fixed dataset.

It is a loop:

    Intelligence
      → action
      → outcome
      → proprietary feedback / memory
      → better next allocation
      → more valuable action

This can create path dependence.

The loop must still be tested for exclusivity and replicability.

## 9. Personal extension

The framework was then applied to an individual.

The traditional question:

> How much is one hour of my labor worth?

was replaced with:

> How much machine intelligence, capital and organization can one hour of my judgment direct?

Potential personal complements were identified:

- Memory;
- Capital;
- Distribution;
- Relationships;
- Owned Businesses;
- Agent Organization.

The proposed transition:

    Human Worker
      → AI Operator
      → Organization Owner
      → Capital Allocator

The practical conclusion was not "learn more AI tools."

It was:

> Build durable assets on which future intelligence can act.

## 10. Immediate personal operating implication

A concrete experiment emerged:

Use a persistent AI organization to operate several real assets — for example an AI-native business, a content/distribution portfolio, and investment research — then review the allocation of attention, capital and intelligence.

The system should answer:

- What produced durable value?
- What merely consumed tokens?
- Which asset has the highest marginal return?
- Where should the next unit of attention and intelligence go?

This is effectively a personal capital-allocation loop.

## 11. Current synthesis

The strongest synthesis as of 2026-10-06 is:

> Intelligence production is becoming only the first half of the economic problem. The second half — intelligence allocation into scarce, defensible, high-value sinks — may determine where durable economic rents accrue.

And at the individual level:

> The model is the engine. The durable asset base determines what that engine can own, remember, reach and compound.

## 12. Open research program

The next research agenda is to test this framework against:

- AI-native model companies;
- cloud / internet companies;
- open-source ecosystems;
- compute factories;
- digital sink owners;
- physical sink owners such as Tesla and Xiaomi;
- personal AI organizations.

The purpose is not to defend the thesis.

The purpose is to discover where it breaks.
