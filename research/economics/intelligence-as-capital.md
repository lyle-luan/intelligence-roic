# Intelligence as Capital

> Status: Working economic interpretation

## 1. Why treat intelligence as a production factor?

Machine intelligence differs from traditional labor because it can potentially be copied, routed, run in parallel, upgraded centrally, embedded in software, connected to tools, connected to physical machines, and accumulated through memory and feedback.

It also differs from ordinary fixed capital because much of it can be consumed on demand.

The useful framing is therefore not "AI is labor" or "AI is capital" in a strict accounting sense. It is:

> Intelligence is a scalable production input whose economics depend on complementary capital and agency.

## 2. Scarcity migration

Suppose the cost of generic intelligence falls sharply.

Then value may migrate toward complementary scarce factors:

- trusted customer access;
- proprietary memory;
- physical endpoints;
- distribution;
- energy;
- compute at constrained moments;
- workflow ownership;
- transaction rights;
- brands;
- regulation / licenses;
- financial capital;
- human judgment in high-consequence decisions.

This resembles earlier technology waves in which the cheapening of one input increased the return to complementary assets.

## 3. Firm boundary implications

Cheap intelligence may change what is economical to internalize.

A firm can increasingly perform tasks internally that previously required external agencies, consultants, contractors, outsourced research, manual coordination, or expensive bespoke software.

But the reverse may also occur: if intelligence becomes a utility, some capabilities become easier to buy from the market.

Therefore AI does not mechanically make firms larger or smaller. It changes the relative transaction cost of internal versus external production.

## 4. New activity, not only substitution

The economic upside should not be measured only by human labor replaced.

Cheaper intelligence can make previously uneconomic activities viable:

- much more software;
- deeper personalization;
- more experiments;
- more research branches;
- more frequent decisions;
- long-tail services;
- new physical automation.

This means AI value can appear as **new activity volume**, not just cost reduction.

## 5. Surplus distribution

The central macro question is:

> Who captures the productivity surplus?

Possible recipients include model labs, chip companies, cloud providers, energy producers, application companies, endpoint owners, enterprises, workers, consumers and governments.

The largest technical contribution does not automatically imply the largest economic rent.

## 6. Research implication

Do not search for a single "super app" as proof of AI return.

Instead observe:

- unit intelligence cost;
- task success;
- human intervention;
- production volume;
- revenue and cost change;
- capital intensity;
- utilization;
- incremental economic profit;
- distribution of surplus.

The real signal is a sustained rise in economic output per unit of scarce capital, not model popularity.
